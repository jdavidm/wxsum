\section{Introduction}

The expanding availability and decreasing cost of use have made weather data, particularly remotely sensed and/or gridded data, a common input into socioeconomic analysis \citep{GortanEtAl24, WuepperEtAl25}. Economists use these ready-made Earth observation products to predict a range of economic outcomes, including agricultural and industrial output, labor productivity, energy demand, health, conflict, human capital, and economic growth. Yet, there are many ways to quantify weather and very little theoretical or systematic guidance on which weather metric is best suited to a given research question and geographic context. The result is that seemingly similar measures of ``rainfall,'' ``temperature,'' or ``shock'' can be based on very different summaries of the underlying weather data \citep{MichlerEtAl26Mining}. The variety of metrics used and the size of weather data frames can create data-handling and cleaning problems, increasing the probability of coding errors when calculating any given metric.

In this article, we introduce the community-contributed command \texttt{wxsum}, which processes daily precipitation and temperature data and produces daily, monthly, seasonal, or annual summary statistics and shock measures. The goal of the command is to enable easy, error-free calculation of the most common weather metrics used in the socioeconomic literature. The \texttt{wxsum} command takes in daily precipitation or temperature data, typically in the form of gridded Earth observation data, and calculates a number of summary statistics for both, such as the mean, median, variance, and skew, as well as phenomenon-specific statistics, such as total rainfall, maximum temperature, and growing degree days (GDD), and several measures of shocks, including deviations from long-run averages, z-scores, dry spells, and heat spells. 

The \texttt{wxsum} command allows the user to customize the dates and lengths of ``seasonal'' windows, the rainfall threshold defining a rainy day, GDD bounds, extreme heat thresholds, daily-temperature bins, the length of the long-run reference window, and the final output shape (long or wide). This flexibility is important because the relevant exposure period, temperature sensitivity, and baseline climate vary across research questions. For example, a study of crop yields might measure rainfall during the growing season, whereas a study of birth outcomes might measure heat exposure over the nine months preceding birth. The command accommodates these differences. Additionally, a companion command, \texttt{wxprep}, reshapes and pre-processes the data for the user into the need format for use in \texttt{wxsum}.

The choice of metric should follow from the research question and the mechanism under study \citep{MichlerEtAl26}. For agricultural applications, total seasonal rainfall, rainy-day counts, and the lengths of early, middle, and late dry spells capture different dimensions of water availability, particularly when the timing and persistence of rainfall deficits matter for crop yields \citep{MarcosGarciaEtAl24}. Growing degree days, extreme-heat days (killing degree days, KDD), and temperature bins capture the accumulated heat exposure relevant to plant development and damage \citep{SchlenkerRoberts09}.

Beyond agriculture, rainfall deviations from long-run averages and corresponding z-scores capture weather shocks relative to local climatic conditions which can be relevant for household consumption and poverty \citep{AzzarriSignorelli20}, as well as social conflict \citep{HendrixSalehyan12}. Temperature bins, extreme-heat, and heat spells capture heat exposure which can be relevant for prenatal development, \citep{DoyleLeckning25}, student learning \citep{ParkEtAl20}, labor productivity\citep{SomanathanEtAl21}, and residential electricity demand \citep{AuffhammerAroonruengsawat11}. These are not exclusive categories; many applications combine several metrics to capture different dimensions of weather exposure. Additionally, we have developed an implementation for R (\texttt{wxsumR}), extending the same core functionality to users who integrate weather processing into R-based data pipelines.

While the preceding guidance reflects common practice in the literature, the command itself is agnostic about which of the many weather metrics a researcher uses. In creating the \texttt{wxsum} command, we are not endorsing an atheoretical or ad hoc approach to incorporating weather measures into socioeconomic analysis. A growing body of literature raises concerns about the use (and misuse) of weather data, particularly when weather is used to identify exogenous variation \citep{BenamiEtAl26}. These concerns include how gridded weather data is matched with socioeconomic data \citep{MichlerEtAl22}, measurement error in Earth observation products \citep{ProctorEtAl23, JosephsonEtAl26}, and the validity of the assumption that weather is truly exogenous \citep{Sarsons15, Mellon2023-un}. While incorporating weather data into one's analysis is now relatively low cost, a na\"{\i}ve or unexamined use of that data will continue to carry a high cost in terms of the accuracy of one's analysis and results.

\section{Data considerations}

The \texttt{wxsum} command is designed for a common structure of remotely sensed or gridded Earth observation products after they have been matched to locations in an analysis dataset. Each observation (row) is a location, plot, household, firm, or other spatial unit, and each variable (column) is a daily measurement for that unit. The command assumes this spatial matching step has been completed. Before running \texttt{wxsum}, the user should decide which weather product, temporal aggregation, and spatial linking rule are appropriate for the research design. These choices are substantive, not merely computational, as estimates can be sensitive to the source of weather data and to the way gridded observations are linked to socioeconomic observations \citep{AuffhammerEtAl13, MichlerEtAl26}.

The texttt{wxsum} command requires Stata 15 or later and for the data to be in wide format. A dataset with 40 years of daily observations implies roughly 14,600 variables per observation. Stata/BE limits datasets to 2,048 variables, which restricts the command to approximately five years of daily data. Stata/SE supports up to 32,767 variables, and Stata/MP supports up to 120,000. Users working with long time series in Stata/BE may need to split the data into shorter panels, run \texttt{wxsum} on each, and append the results.\footnote{Splitting the data affects the reference period used for calculating deviations and z-scores. Users who split their data should ensure sufficient overlap to avoid truncating the reference period.}

\subsection{Shape and naming convention}

Daily weather values are stored in separate numeric variables whose names share a common prefix (stub) and end with an eight-digit date in \texttt{yyyymmdd} format. For example, if the precipitation variables are named with the prefix \texttt{rf\_}, then precipitation on May 15, 1979, should appear in a variable named \texttt{rf\_19790515}. A similar approach applies for temperature variable names after the prefix. Variables not beginning with the specified stub are ignored unless they are retained using the \texttt{keep()} option.

The companion command \texttt{wxprep} prepares weather data that do not follow this structure. It converts long-format data to the required wide format, renames existing daily variables to follow the date-based naming convention, and aggregates sub-daily readings into daily summaries. It can also convert wide-format data back to long form when users need to perform additional transformations before calculating weather metrics. Data that already satisfy the input requirements can be passed directly to \texttt{wxsum}.

The wide format, while memory-intensive, is a natural representation of the gridded Earth observation products common in the climate science literature. The \texttt{wxsum} command is agnostic to the source of the data. It can process data from any product that provides daily or subdaily readings on a regular grid. Widely used products of this type include CHIRPS \citep{CHIRPS}, CPC \citep{CPC08}, ARC2 \citep{ARC2}, TAMSAT \citep{TAMSAT}, ERA5 \citep{ERA5}, and MERRA-2 \citep{MERRA2}. It can also process data from ground-based weather station networks.\footnote{While designed for temperature and precipitation data, the command's agnosticism means one could pass it any type of daily weather data, such as humidity, wind speed, wet bulb temperature, or standardized precipitation evapotranspiration index (SPEI). However, not every metric that \texttt{wxsum} produces will have a meaningful interpretation when the data is not temperature or precipitation.}

The required naming convention for the input data makes the calendar explicit. The command parses the date suffix of each weather variable, identifies the days that belong to each user-defined season or window, and computes statistics separately for each location and each window. As all computations are based on the dates in variable names, users should check that the date suffixes are complete, unique, and measured consistently on the intended calendar and time zone. The command can accommodate windows that cross calendar years. For example, when a window spans two calendar years, it is assigned to the year in which it begins. A window running from November 2001 to February 2002 is labeled 2001 in the command's output.

\subsection{Windows, units, and missing values}

Users can define the relevant time window in one of three ways. The default is to define a calendar window with an initial month and final month and, optionally, an initial day and final day. This fixes the window to the same start and end date for all units and for all years. Alternatively, the user can define unit-specific windows. These can be defined by providing the command with a variable that holds each observations' start and end date, such as a planting date and harvest date. Or, the window can be defined with a variable that holds an event date for each observations as well as the number of days the event lasted, such as a birth date and the length of the gestation period. In both cases, the exposure window is defined separately for each observation rather than being fixed to the same calendar dates across observations and years. The command does not impose a biological or econometric interpretation on the chosen window. Rather, the user must define the exposure window before running the command and should report that choice in subsequent empirical work.

The command is similarly agnostic about units. Rainfall can be measured in millimeters, centimeters, or inches, and temperature can be measured in degrees Celsius, Fahrenheit, or Kelvin. It is necessary, however, that all daily variables are measured consistently with the same unit used throughout. Unit choices matter for thresholds. For example, the lower and upper bounds for GDD and the base for KDD must be expressed in the same units as the daily temperature data. Similarly, the rainy-day threshold must be expressed in the same units as the daily precipitation data.

Many weather products contain missing values due to sensor problems, limited spatial coverage, cloud contamination, or limitations in station interpolation \citep{Dinku2020}. Users should examine missingness in the daily variables and decide whether the weather product and temporal coverage are adequate for the application. For means, medians, standard deviations, maxima, rainy-day counts, rain-day shares, daily-temperature bin counts, and dry spells and heat spells, missing daily values are not treated as observed weather. For seasonal and monthly precipitation totals, missing daily values contribute zero to those sums. This means seasons with missing days will understate true missingness in total rainfall. The command has an option that allows the user to define the minimum number of non-missing observations that are acceptable in a window. It also produces stored results that capture the degree of missingness in the unprocessed weather data.


\section{The \texttt{wxsum} and \texttt{wxprep} commands}
\label{sec:commands}

The \texttt{wxsum} command calculates weather statistics for calendar or unit-specific windows. The companion command \texttt{wxprep} prepares the daily data by reshaping observations, renaming variables, or aggregating multiple readings within a day. This section describes the syntax, options, output, and stored results of each command.

\subsection{The \texttt{wxsum} command}
\label{sec:wxsum}

\subsubsection{Syntax}

For a calendar window, the same starting and ending dates are applied to every observation, with one window per year:

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxsum {\it stub\/}, type(rain\textbar temp)
	ini\_month(\#) fin\_month(\#)
	[ ini\_day(\#) fin\_day(\#) {\it options\/} ]
\end{stsyntax}

\noindent For a unit-specific window, the starting and ending dates are read from variables, with one window per observation:

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxsum {\it stub\/}, type(rain\textbar temp)
	start({\it varname\/}) end({\it varname\/})
	[ partial {\it options\/} ]
\end{stsyntax}

\noindent Alternatively, a unit-specific window can be defined relative to an event date:

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxsum {\it stub\/}, type(rain\textbar temp)
	event\_date({\it varname\/}) days(\#)
	[ lag(\#) partial {\it options\/} ]
\end{stsyntax}

\noindent Here \textit{stub} is the common prefix for the daily weather variables. It is not a varlist. If the data contain variables named \texttt{rf\_19790101}, \texttt{rf\_19790102}, and so on, the stub supplied to the command is \texttt{rf\_}. The user specifies \texttt{type(rain)} or \texttt{type(temp)} and one of the three window definitions. With \texttt{type(temp)}, \texttt{gdd\_lo()}, \texttt{gdd\_hi()}, and \texttt{heat\_threshold()} are also required. The remaining options are described below.

\subsubsection{Required variables}

Input data must be wide, with one row per observation and separate numeric variables for daily weather values. Each daily variable name consists of the supplied stub followed by a date in \texttt{yyyymmdd} format. A calendar day without a corresponding variable is treated as missing. Unit-specific windows additionally require variables containing Stata daily dates. Identifiers are not arguments of \texttt{wxsum}, but can be retained with \texttt{keep()} for linking the results to other data. Table~\ref{tab:datareq} summarizes the input requirements.

\subsubsection{Options}

\noindent\textit{Calendar windows}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{ini\_month(\#)} and \texttt{fin\_month(\#)}] specify the first and last month of a calendar window, from 1 through 12. Both are required for this window definition. When the final month precedes the initial month, the window crosses the end of the calendar year and is associated with the year in which it starts.
	\item[\texttt{ini\_day(\#)} and \texttt{fin\_day(\#)}] specify the first and last day within those months. By default, the window begins on the first day of the initial month and ends on the last day of the final month, including February 29 in leap years. If February 29 is explicitly specified, February 28 is used in common years.
\end{description}

\noindent\textit{Unit-specific windows}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{start(varname)} and \texttt{end(varname)}] identify variables containing the first and last day of each observation's window as Stata daily dates (\texttt{\%td}). Windows can differ in length for each observation. An observation with a missing date receives missing statistics. Datetimes in \texttt{\%tc} format are not accepted directly; they must first be converted to daily dates using \texttt{dofc()} or the \texttt{convert()} option of \texttt{wxprep}.
	\item[\texttt{event\_date(varname)} and \texttt{days(\#)}] define a window of \texttt{\#} days ending the day before an event. Windows have a common length but the event-date variable allows the dates to differ across observations.
	\item[\texttt{lag(\#)}] shifts an event-based window by the specified number of days. The default is 0. A positive value moves the window earlier and a negative value moves it later. The resulting start and end dates are \texttt{event\_date - days - lag} and \texttt{event\_date - 1 - lag}, respectively.
	\item[\texttt{partial}] uses the available days when the window extends before the first or after the last date of the daily variables for a given unit of observation. Without this option, such windows receive missing statistics. This option does not change the calendar-window requirement that the entire window lie within the data's date range.
\end{description}

\noindent With either unit-specific definition, the output contains one row per input observation and generated variable names have no year suffix. Because each observation has only one exposure window, the \texttt{shape()} option does not apply.

\noindent\textit{Rainfall and temperature thresholds}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{rain\_threshold(\#)}] defines a rainy day as one with precipitation at or above the threshold and a dry day as one with precipitation below it. The default is 1. This option applies only to \texttt{type(rain)}.
	\item[\texttt{gdd\_lo(\#)} and \texttt{gdd\_hi(\#)}] specify the lower and upper bounds for GDD. Both are required with \texttt{type(temp)}.
	\item[\texttt{heat\_threshold(\#)}] defines a heat day as one with temperature at or above the threshold. It is required with \texttt{type(temp)} and is used to calculate heat-day counts and heat spells. It also sets the KDD base unless \texttt{kdd\_base()} is specified. The interpretation depends on the supplied daily series: a threshold applied to daily mean temperature measures a different exposure from the same threshold applied to daily maximum temperature.
	\item[\texttt{kdd\_base(\#)}] optionally specifies a separate base for KDD. Without this option, the heat threshold is also the KDD base.
\end{description}

\noindent All thresholds must use the units of the daily data. The command does not convert units.

\noindent\textit{Optional bins and categories}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{gdd\_bin(\#)}] specifies the width (in GDD units) of GDD bins. When specified, the command creates \texttt{gddcat}, an integer category identifying the fixed-width interval containing the window's GDD total. Categories are pooled across observations and windows and have value labels identifying the intervals, so \texttt{i.gddcat} can be used in regressions. This option applies only to \texttt{type(temp)}.
	\item[\texttt{gdd\_binlo(\#)}] specifies the lower endpoint of the regular GDD intervals when \texttt{gdd\_bin()} is used. The default is 0. Totals below this endpoint receive a bottom-coded category.
	\item[\texttt{gdd\_binhi(\#)}] specifies the upper endpoint of the regular GDD intervals. Totals at or above it receive a top-coded category. When it is omitted, the intervals extend beyond the largest GDD total. The difference between the upper and lower endpoints must be a multiple of the width specified in \texttt{gdd\_bin()}.
	\item[\texttt{tmp\_bin(\#)}] specifies the number of daily temperature bins, from 1 through 42. It requires \texttt{tmp\_binlo()} and \texttt{tmp\_binhi()}. For three or more bins, the first counts days below the lower endpoint, the last counts days at or above the upper endpoint, and the remaining bins divide the interval between the endpoints into equal widths. Interior bins include their lower boundary and exclude their upper boundary. \texttt{tmp\_bin(1)} counts all observed days; \texttt{tmp\_bin(2)} divides them at the midpoint of the two endpoints. The generated variables are named \texttt{tmpbin01}, \texttt{tmpbin02}, and so on.
	\item[\texttt{tmp\_binlo(\#)} and \texttt{tmp\_binhi(\#)}] specify the lower and upper endpoints. These are absolute temperature thresholds, not percentiles of the observed distribution.
	\item[\texttt{cuts(numlist)}] creates daily bin counts using up to 41 increasing cutpoints. The first variable, \texttt{cut01}, counts days below the first cutpoint. Subsequent variables count days from one cutpoint up to, but not including, the next. The final variable counts days at or above the last cutpoint. Thus $K$ cutpoints create $K + 1$ bins. This option can be used with either data type, for example to define precipitation-intensity or temperature-exposure bands. Cannot be combined with \texttt{tmp\_bin()}.
\end{description}

\noindent Daily bins count observed days rather than shares of days. Their values are missing when a window has fewer observed days than required by \texttt{minobs()}. Output names receive a year suffix only for calendar windows with wide output, as described below.

\noindent\textit{Reference periods}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{lr\_years(\#)}] specifies a rolling reference period consisting of windows starting in the indicated number of preceding years. The permitted range is 2 through 50 and the default is 10. The current window is excluded from its own rolling reference period.
	\item[\texttt{baseline(\# \#)}] specifies a fixed reference period. For example, \texttt{baseline(1981 2010)} uses windows starting in 1981 through 2010. Windows inside the baseline are compared with a baseline that includes them. The baseline must cover at least \texttt{ref\_min()} windows of the data, or years of the data for unit-specific windows. Cannot be combined with \texttt{lr\_years()}.
	\item[\texttt{ref\_min(\#)}] specifies the minimum number of nonmissing reference values an observation needs for a deviation or $z$-score. It must be at least 2. By default, all reference years are required: the number specified in \texttt{lr\_years()} or the number of years in \texttt{baseline()}.
\end{description}

\noindent For unit-specific windows, the reference values are calculated for the same window moved back to each preceding year or to each baseline year. February 29 is moved to February 28 in common years. The reference period and \texttt{ref\_min()} govern deviations and $z$-scores rather than the nonreference summaries of the current window.

\noindent\textit{Missing data}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{minobs(\#)}] specifies the minimum number of nonmissing daily observations required to calculate statistics for a window (default: 1). If this requirement is not met, all statistics are set to missing.
\end{description}

\noindent\textit{Output}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{shape(long\textbar wide)}] specifies the output shape for calendar windows. The default, \texttt{long}, produces one row per observation and window, a variable named \texttt{year} identifying the window's starting year, and statistic names without year suffixes. \texttt{wide} produces one row per observation and statistic names with year suffixes. Input data must be wide in either case. This option has no effect on unit-specific windows.
	\item[\texttt{outstub(string)}] adds a prefix to every generated variable name except \texttt{year}. For example, \texttt{outstub(r\_)} gives \texttt{r\_total}. Separate output prefixes allow rainfall and temperature results, or results for different windows, to be kept together.
    \item[\texttt{keep(varlist)}] specifies which variables from the input dataset are retained in the output. For calendar windows with \texttt{shape(long)}, the output contains only the generated statistics and \texttt{year} by default. The \texttt{keep()} option allows users to retain additional variables, such as identifiers. For calendar windows with \texttt{shape(wide)} and for unit-specific windows, all input variables are retained by default. Specifying \texttt{keep()} restricts the output to the listed input variables and the generated statistics.
	\item[\texttt{save(filename [, replace])}] saves the output dataset and leaves the results in memory. To overwrite an existing file, specify \texttt{replace} within the option, as in \texttt{save(rain\_out, replace)}.
\end{description}


\subsubsection{Output}

\texttt{wxsum} is a data-management command that produces a dataset of window-level weather statistics. For calendar windows, the default \texttt{shape(long)} output contains one row per observation and year, with a variable named \texttt{year} identifying the window. With \texttt{shape(wide)}, each input observation remains one row and the generated statistics receive year suffixes, such as \texttt{mean\_1993} and \texttt{total\_1993}. Windows crossing calendar years are assigned to the year in which they begin. For unit-specific windows, the output contains one row per input observation, and generated statistics have no year suffix. Table~\ref{tab:rainout} and~\ref{tab:tempout} list rainfall and temperature statistics without an optional output prefix or year suffix.

For both rainfall and temperature, the command calculates deviations and $z$-scores for selected statistics relative to a rolling or fixed reference period. These measures distinguish weather exposure from conditions that are unusual relative to the historical experience of a location. The variables \texttt{pct\_raindays} and \texttt{pct\_heatdays} report shares, not percentages. Both outputs include \texttt{nobs}, the number of nonmissing daily observations, to help assess data completeness.

Finally, the generated variables include labels identifying the statistics and relevant thresholds. Variable and dataset characteristics record the exposure windows and command settings, providing a record of how the weather measures were constructed that persists when the output dataset is saved.

\subsection{The \texttt{wxprep} command}
\label{sec:wxprep}

The \texttt{wxprep} command prepares the daily data required by \texttt{wxsum}. It has three operations: \texttt{towide} converts long-format observations to daily wide-format variables; \texttt{rename} changes existing wide-format variable names to the required date-based convention; and \texttt{tolong} converts daily wide-format variables back to long observations. The last operation allows users to carry out transformations, such as unit conversions or combining daily series, before returning to wide form.

\subsubsection{Syntax}

To convert long data, with one row per unit-day or per unit-reading, to wide data, the syntax is

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxprep towide {\it varname\/} [ if {\it exp\/} ] [ in {\it range\/} ],
	id({\it varlist\/}) date({\it varname\/}) stub({\it string\/})
	[ daily(mean\textbar sum\textbar min\textbar max) dateformat({\it string\/})
	nofill convert({\it varlist\/}) clear
	save({\it filename\/} [, replace]) ]
\end{stsyntax}

\noindent The first \textit{varname} identifies the numeric weather series. \texttt{id()} identifies the units, \texttt{date()} identifies the date variable, and \texttt{stub()} supplies the prefix for the new daily variables. The \texttt{if} and \texttt{in} qualifiers can restrict which observations are converted.

For wide data whose daily variables represent consecutive dates in a specified order, the syntax is

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxprep rename {\it varlist\/}, stub({\it string\/}) firstdate({\it date\/})
	[ convert({\it varlist\/}) ]
\end{stsyntax}

\noindent For wide data whose variable names contain dates after an old prefix, the syntax is

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxprep rename {\it varlist\/}, stub({\it string\/})
	from({\it string\/}) dateformat({\it string\/})
	[ convert({\it varlist\/}) ]
\end{stsyntax}

To convert daily wide-format variables to one row per unit-day, the syntax is

\begin{stsyntax}
	wxprep tolong {\it stub\/}, id({\it varlist\/})
	[ generate({\it newvar\/}) date({\it newvar\/})
	convert({\it varlist\/}) clear save({\it filename\/} [, replace]) ]
\end{stsyntax}

\noindent In \texttt{tolong}, \textit{stub} identifies the existing daily weather variables. The meanings of \texttt{date()}, \texttt{id()}, and \texttt{convert()} depend on the operation, as described below.

\subsubsection{Options}

\noindent\textit{Options for \texttt{wxprep towide}}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{id(varlist)}] identifies the units and is required. The identifier variables can be numeric or string, and each distinct combination becomes one output row. Missing identifiers are not allowed. Apart from the daily variables it creates, \texttt{towide} retains only the \texttt{id()} variables.
	\item[\texttt{date(varname)}] identifies the date of each reading and is required. It accepts Stata daily dates (\texttt{\%td}), datetimes (\texttt{\%tc} or \texttt{\%tC}, retaining the day), integers in \texttt{yyyymmdd} form, or string dates accompanied by \texttt{dateformat()}.
	\item[\texttt{stub(string)}] specifies the prefix of the new daily variables and is required. It is used exactly as typed and can contain at most 24 characters. For example, \texttt{stub(rf\_)} produces names such as \texttt{rf\_19930515}.
	\item[\texttt{daily(mean\textbar sum\textbar min\textbar max)}] combines multiple readings for the same unit and day using their mean, sum, minimum, or maximum. Examples include \texttt{daily(mean)} for daily mean temperature, \texttt{daily(sum)} for accumulated daily precipitation, and \texttt{daily(max)} for daily maximum temperature. Missing readings are ignored. Without \texttt{daily()}, duplicate unit-day readings produce an error.
	\item[\texttt{dateformat(string)}] gives the order of year, month, and day in a string date. For example, the mask \texttt{YMD} reads \texttt{2020-01-31}, whereas \texttt{DMY} reads \texttt{31jan2020}.
	\item[\texttt{nofill}] creates variables only for dates appearing in the data. By default, \texttt{towide} creates a variable for every calendar day between the first and last date, leaving days without readings missing. These are missing days, not zero weather values.
	\item[\texttt{convert(varlist)}] converts additional datetime variables to daily dates by removing the time of day. Variable names and labels are preserved. The listed variables must also appear in \texttt{id()} so they are retained in the output. The weather-reading datetime supplied in \texttt{date()} is converted automatically and does not need to be listed here. The converted dates can then define unit-specific exposure windows in \texttt{wxsum}. Conversion occurs before grouping, so records distinguished only by different times on the same day will be treated as belonging to the same unit.
	\item[\texttt{clear}] permits replacement of data in memory that have changed since they were last saved.
	\item[\texttt{save(filename [, replace])}] saves the wide-format output; \texttt{replace} permits overwriting an existing file.
\end{description}

\noindent\textit{Options for \texttt{wxprep rename}}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{stub(string)}] specifies the prefix of the new names and is required.
	\item[\texttt{firstdate(date)}] specifies the date of the first variable when the listed variables represent consecutive days in the order supplied. Accepted representations include \texttt{01jan1993}, \texttt{1993-01-01}, and \texttt{19930101}.
	\item[\texttt{from(string)} and \texttt{dateformat(string)}] are used instead of \texttt{firstdate()} when each existing name contains its date. \texttt{from()} identifies the old prefix and \texttt{dateformat()} specifies the order of the date components that follow it. For names such as \texttt{rain\_01jan1993}, use the old prefix \texttt{rain\_} with the mask \texttt{DMY}.
	\item[\texttt{convert(varlist)}] converts \texttt{\%tc} or \texttt{\%tC} datetimes to \texttt{\%td} daily dates, preserving names and labels. With \texttt{rename}, these can be any variables other than the daily weather variables being renamed.
\end{description}

\noindent Before making changes, \texttt{rename} checks that the dates are valid, no two variables refer to the same date, and the new variable names do not already exist. It reports gaps between the first and last date.

\noindent\textit{Options for \texttt{wxprep tolong}}

\begin{description}
	\item[\texttt{id(varlist)}] is required and must uniquely identify the input observations. Variables other than the identifiers and the daily weather series are dropped. Unit-level variables that should be retained must therefore be included in \texttt{id()}.
	\item[\texttt{generate(newvar)}] names the new variable holding the daily weather values. The default is the stub with a trailing underscore removed; for example, \texttt{rf\_} gives \texttt{rf}. The option can be abbreviated to \texttt{gen()}.
	\item[\texttt{date(newvar)}] names the new Stata daily-date variable. The default name is \texttt{date}, with format \texttt{\%td}. Unlike \texttt{date()} in \texttt{towide}, this option names an output variable rather than identifying an input variable.
	\item[\texttt{convert(varlist)}] converts \texttt{\%tc} or \texttt{\%tC} datetime variables to \texttt{\%td} daily dates, preserving names and labels. The variables must appear in \texttt{id()}, and the identifiers must remain unique after conversion.
	\item[\texttt{clear}] permits replacement of data in memory that have changed since they were last saved.
	\item[\texttt{save(filename [, replace])}] saves the long-format output; \texttt{replace} permits overwriting an existing file.
\end{description}

\section{Formulas and Calculations}

This section defines the variables generated by \texttt{wxsum}. Let $i$ index the observation in the dataset and let $t$ denote the year in which a season begins. Let $S_t$ be the set of daily dates included in the season beginning in year $t$, and let $N_{it}$ be the number of non-missing daily observations for observation $i$ in season $t$. For rainfall variables, let $R_{id}$ denote daily precipitation on date $d$; for temperature variables, let $T_{id}$ denote daily temperature. The command creates variables with the year suffix $\_\textit{YYYY}$ but in order to simplify notation in this section, we let $\_t$ stand for the year, so the formulas below describe, for example, \texttt{mean\_}$t$ instead of $\texttt{mean\_}\textit{YYYY}$.

\subsection{Statistics common to rainfall and temperature}

For either rainfall or temperature data, write $X_{id}$ for the relevant daily variable, either $R_{id}$ or $T_{id}$. The daily sample mean is

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{mean\_} t_i = \frac{1}{N_{it}} \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ X_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} X_{id}.
\end{equation}

The daily median, \texttt{median\_}$t$, is the median of the non-missing values $\{X_{id} : d \in S_t, X_{id} \neq \text{missing}\}$. The daily variance, \texttt{var\_}$t$, requires at least 2 non-missing observations and is the sample variance of those same non-missing values,

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{var\_} t_i = \frac{1}{N_{it} - 1} \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ X_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \left(X_{id} - \texttt{mean\_} t_i \right)^2 .
\end{equation}

The daily standard deviation, \texttt{sd\_}$t$, is the square root of the variance. The skewness variable is the adjusted Fisher-Pearson sample skewness,

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{skew\_} t_i = \frac{N_{it}}{(N_{it}-1)(N_{it}-2)} \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ X_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \left( \frac{X_{id} - \texttt{mean\_} t_i}{\texttt{sd\_} t_i} \right)^3 .
\end{equation}

\noindent The third moment variable requires at least 3 non-missing observations. When $N_{it} < 3$ or the standard deviation is zero, the skewness variable is missing.

\subsection{Rainfall calculations}

For precipitation data, the seasonal total is

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{total\_} t_i = \sum_{d \in S_t} R_{id},
\end{equation}

\noindent where the implementation follows \texttt{rowtotal()} and therefore treats missing daily rainfall components as zero in the sum.

Monthly totals are computed first for each calendar month $m$ represented in the selected season,

\begin{equation}
	M_{imt} = \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ \text{month} (d) = m}} R_{id}.
\end{equation}

\noindent The variable \texttt{mean\_mo\_}$t$ is the mean of the monthly totals $M_{imt}$ across the $k_{it}$ months represented in the season.

Let $c$ denote the value supplied in \texttt{rain\_threshold()}. Rainy days and days without rain are counted only among non-missing daily rainfall values:

\begin{align}
	\texttt{raindays\_} t_i & = \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ R_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \mathbf{1}\{R_{id} \geq c\}, \\
	\texttt{norain\_} t_i & = \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ R_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \mathbf{1}\{R_{id} < c\}.
\end{align}

\noindent The share of observed days with rain is

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{pct\_raindays\_} t_i = \frac{\texttt{raindays\_} t_i}{N_{it}}.
\end{equation}

The command calculate three different dry spells. The variable \texttt{dry\_}$t$ is the longest mid-season dry spell: the maximum run of consecutive non-missing days with $R_{id} < c$ that occurs strictly after the first observed rainy day and strictly before the last observed rainy day in the season. Leading dry days before the first rainy day and trailing dry days after the last rainy day are excluded from \texttt{dry\_}$t$.

The variable \texttt{dry\_start\_}$t$ is the number of consecutive non-missing dry days at the start of the season before the first rainy day or missing value. The variable \texttt{dry\_end\_}$t$ is the number of consecutive non-missing dry days at the end of the season after the last rainy day or missing value, counted backward from the final day of the season. If all daily rainfall values in a location-season are missing, all three dry-spell variables are missing. Missing rainfall values break dry spells rather than extending them.

\subsection{Temperature calculations}

For temperature data, the maximum is:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{max\_} t_i = \max_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ T_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} T_{id}.
\end{equation}

Let $a$ be the lower bound supplied in \texttt{gdd\_lo()} and $b$ be the upper bound supplied in \texttt{gdd\_hi()}. Daily growing degree days are truncated below at zero and above at $b - a$, and seasonal GDD are accumulated as:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{gdd\_} t_i = \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ T_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \min \left\{ \max \left(T_{id} - a, 0 \right), b - a \right\}.
\end{equation}

If the user supplies a positive value $k$ in \texttt{kdd\_base()}, killing degree days are:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{kdd\_} t_i = \sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ T_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \max \left(T_{id} - k, 0 \right).
\end{equation}

\noindent If \texttt{kdd\_base()} is left at its default value of zero, \texttt{wxsum} does not generate the KDD variables.

GDD bins provide a nonlinear degree-day specification in the spirit of \citet{DescheneGreenstone07}. This generated categorical variable identifies which fixed-width GDD interval the accumulated seasonal GDD total falls into. When \texttt{gdd\_bin()} is specified, let $\text{GDD}_{it}$ denote seasonal growing degree-days computed as above, and let $\tau_0 < \tau_1 < \cdots < \tau_J$ denote fixed GDD cutpoints generated from \texttt{gdd\_bin()}, \texttt{gdd\_binlo()}, and either \texttt{gdd\_binhi()} or the automatically determined upper endpoint. For regular categories:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{gddcat}_{it} = j \quad \text{if} \quad \tau_{j-1} \leq \text{GDD}_{it} < \tau_j.
\end{equation}

\noindent If a bottom-coded bin is needed because some seasonal GDD totals fall below \texttt{gdd\_binlo()}:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{gddcat}_{it} = 1 \quad \text{if} \quad \text{GDD}_{it} < \tau_0.
\end{equation}

\noindent If a top-coded bin is requested via \texttt{gdd\_binhi()}:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{gddcat}_{it} = J + 1 \quad \text{if} \quad \text{GDD}_{it} \geq \tau_J.
\end{equation}

\noindent The variable \texttt{gddcat\_}\textit{YYYY} is an integer-valued categorical variable with value labels define the GDD intervals, so that \texttt{tabulate gddcat\_}\textit{YYYY} displays the interval boundaries. Users can employ factor-variable notation, such as \texttt{i.gddcat\_}\textit{YYYY} in wide output or \texttt{i.gddcat} after \texttt{shape(long)}, to create regression indicators for each bin. The command is unit agnostic so \texttt{gdd\_bin()}, \texttt{gdd\_binlo()}, and \texttt{gdd\_binhi()} must be expressed in the units of the generated GDD variable.

Daily temperature bins are another nonlinear measure that summarizes the seasonal distribution of observed daily temperatures across common absolute temperature intervals in the spirit of \cite{SchlenkerRoberts09}. These variables count the number of nonmissing daily temperature readings falling in each interval and are created when \texttt{tmp\_bin()} is specified.

For $J = \texttt{tmp\_bin()}$ with $J \geq 3$, let $\ell = \texttt{tmp\_binlo()}$, $h = \texttt{tmp\_binhi()}$, and $w = (h - \ell) / (J - 2)$. The bin counts are:

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{tmpbin}_{it}^{(j)} =
	\begin{cases}
		\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ T_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \mathbf{1}\{T_{id} < \ell\}, & j = 1, \\[8pt]
		\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ T_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \mathbf{1}\{\ell + (j - 2)w \leq T_{id} < \ell + (j - 1)w\}, & 2 \leq j \leq J - 1, \\[8pt]
		\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{d \in \mathcal{S}_{t} \\ T_{id} \neq \text{missing}}} \mathbf{1}\{T_{id} \geq h\}, & j = J.
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent Interior intervals are lower-closed and upper-open. The lower tail is strictly below $\ell$; the upper tail is at or above $h$. The generated variable names are zero-padded, for example \texttt{tmpbin01\_1993}.

Two special cases apply for small $J$. When \texttt{tmp\_bin(1)} is specified, a single variable \texttt{tmpbin01\_}\textit{YYYY} counts all nonmissing daily temperature readings in the season. When \texttt{tmp\_bin(2)} is specified, the command splits at the midpoint $m = (\ell + h) / 2$: \texttt{tmpbin01\_}\textit{YYYY} counts days with $T < m$ and \texttt{tmpbin02\_}\textit{YYYY} counts days with $T \geq m$. Missing daily temperatures are not counted in any bin. If all daily temperatures for a location-season are missing, all bin variables for that location-season are set to missing. Otherwise, the sum of all bin counts equals the number of nonmissing daily temperature readings in the season.

\subsection{Long-run reference periods}

Several variables generated by \texttt{wxsum} describe weather shocks as deviations from a location-specific long-run reference period. Let $w_{it}$ be one of the seasonal weather statistics for which a long-run benchmark is generated: \texttt{total\_}$t$, \texttt{raindays\_}$t$, \texttt{norain\_}$t$, or \texttt{pct\_raindays\_}$t$ for rainfall data; \texttt{gdd\_}$t$ and \texttt{kdd\_}$t$ for temperature data. Let $L$ be the number of prior years requested in \texttt{lr\_years()}. The rolling long-run mean and standard deviation used for deviations and $z$ scores are based only on strictly preceding seasons:

\begin{equation}
	\bar{w}_{it}^{L} = L^{-1} \sum_{l = 1}^{L} w_{i, t - l}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	s_{it}^{L} = \left\{(L - 1)^{-1} \sum_{l = 1}^{L} \left(w_{i, t - l} - \bar{w}_{it}^{L} \right)^2 \right\}^{1 / 2} .
\end{equation}

The deviation and corresponding $z$ score are

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{dev\_} w\texttt{\_} t_i = w_{it} - \bar{w}_{it}^{L} ,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\texttt{z\_} w\texttt{\_} t_i = \frac{w_{it} - \bar{w}_{it}^{L}}{s_{it}^{L}} .
\end{equation}

\noindent The use of strictly prior years avoids including the current realization in its own benchmark. If there are too few preceding season variables to satisfy the requested value of \texttt{lr\_years()}, the long-run deviations and $z$ scores are not generated for those initial seasons.

\section{Examples}

The package includes small rainfall and temperature datasets that can be used to check installation and illustrate the syntax. These files are also useful as templates for checking the required wide layout and variable-naming conventions. 

\subsection{Rainfall summaries}

Suppose that the daily rainfall variables in \texttt{rain.dta} are named with the prefix \texttt{rf\_}. The following commands calculate seasonal rainfall statistics for a May 15 through October 15 growing season and save the resulting dataset:

\begin{stlog}
	. use rain.dta, clear
	. wxsum rf_, type(rain) ini_month(05) fin_month(10) ini_day(15) ///
	>     fin_day(15) rain_threshold(1) keep(hhid) ///
	>     save(rainfall_stats.dta)
\end{stlog}

\noindent The output dataset contains one observation for each input location. For each season observed in the daily rainfall variables, \texttt{wxsum} adds the rainfall statistics listed in Table~\ref{tab:rainout}. If the user also needs location identifiers or other merge variables, these should be supplied in \texttt{keep()}. The resulting file can then be merged with a socioeconomic dataset using the retained identifier variables and the season-specific weather variables generated.

\subsection{Temperature summaries}

Suppose that the daily temperature variables in \texttt{temp.dta} are named with the prefix \texttt{tmp\_}. The following commands calculate seasonal temperature statistics for a November through February growing season. As the final month precedes the initial month, the command treats the exposure window as spanning calendar years and assigns the season to the year in which it begins:

\begin{stlog}
	. use temp.dta, clear
	. wxsum tmp_, type(temp) ini_month(11) fin_month(02) gdd_lo(8)  ///
	>     gdd_hi(32) kdd_base(32) keep(hhid)
\end{stlog}

\noindent This command generates the temperature summaries listed in Table~\ref{tab:tempout}. The values \texttt{gdd\_lo(8)}, \texttt{gdd\_hi(32)}, and \texttt{kdd\_base(32)} are illustrative; they should be chosen to match the units of the underlying temperature data and the biological or behavioral process being studied.

\subsection{GDD and temperature bins}

To create Desch{\^e}nes--Greenstone-style GDD bins, the user specifies \texttt{gdd\_bin()} with a bin width in the units of the generated GDD variable:

\begin{stlog}
	. use temp.dta, clear
	. wxsum tmp_, type(temp) ini_month(04) ini_day(01) fin_month(09) ///
	>     fin_day(15) gdd_lo(18) gdd_hi(30) kdd_base(32) gdd_bin(100) ///
	>     gdd_binlo(50) gdd_binhi(950) keep(hhid)
\end{stlog}

\noindent Here \texttt{gdd\_bin(100)} means the bin width will be 100 degree-days in the units of the generated GDD variable. In this example the user set \texttt{gdd\_binhi()} and \texttt{gdd\_binlo()} though these are not required. In the absence of these options, the default lower endpoint is 0 while the command determines the upper endpoint from the pooled empirical maximum of GDD. This example assumes Celsius temperatures and Celsius degree-days. The command does not infer or convert units.

To create Schlenker--Roberts-style temperature bins, the user specifies \texttt{tmp\_bin()} along with \texttt{tmp\_binlo()} and \texttt{tmp\_binhi()}:

\begin{stlog}
	. use temp.dta, clear
	. wxsum tmp_, type(temp) ini_month(04) ini_day(01) fin_month(09) ///
	>     fin_day(15) gdd_lo(18) gdd_hi(30) kdd_base(32) tmp_bin(15)  ///
	>     tmp_binlo(0) tmp_binhi(39) keep(hhid)
\end{stlog}

\noindent Here \texttt{tmp\_bin(15)}, \texttt{tmp\_binlo(0)}, and \texttt{tmp\_binhi(39)} create 15 bin-count variables per season: a lower tail counting days below 0, 13 equal-width interior intervals over $[0, 39)$, and an upper tail counting days at or above 39. This is a Celsius-style example; the command does not infer or convert units.

\subsection{Changing the long-run benchmark}

The default long-run reference period is 10 prior years. Researchers working with longer weather histories may prefer a longer benchmark, whereas applications with short panels may require a shorter one. The following command uses the prior 20 years as the rolling reference period:

\begin{stlog}
	. use rain.dta, clear
	. wxsum rf_, type(rain) ini_month(05) fin_month(10) lr_years(20) ///
	>     keep(hhid)
\end{stlog}

\noindent Changing \texttt{lr\_years()} affects only the rolling deviations and $z$-scores. It does not affect the seasonal means, medians, variances, standard deviations, skewness measures, totals, counts, shares, dry-spell measures, GDD categories, or daily-temperature bin counts.

\subsection{Long output}

To produce long-shaped output, the user specifies \texttt{shape(long)}:

\begin{stlog}
	. use rain.dta, clear
	. wxsum rf_, type(rain) ini_month(05) fin_month(10) ini_day(15) ///
	>     fin_day(15) rain_threshold(1) keep(hhid) ///
	>     shape(long)
\end{stlog}

\noindent The output has one row per \texttt{id}-year. The time variable is named \texttt{year}. Variables are named \texttt{mean}, \texttt{median}, and so on, rather than \texttt{mean\_}\textit{YYYY}, \texttt{median\_}\textit{YYYY}, etc.


\section{Conclusions}

\texttt{wxsum} reduces the amount of custom code needed to transform daily weather data into seasonal measures commonly used in socioeconomic analysis.  It standardizes the construction of summary statistics, threshold-based measures, rainy-day counts, dry spells, GDD bins, daily temperature bins, and rolling weather-shock measures. The command handles cross-year seasons, missing data, and large datasets without requiring users to reshape or preprocess their data beyond the standard wide format. An implementation for R has also been developed, extending the same core functionality to users who work outside Stata or who integrate weather processing into R-based data pipelines.

While standardizing calculations, \texttt{wxsum} leaves substantive choices with the researcher by allowing users to customize the season definition, the thresholds for growing and killing degree days, GDD bin widths and endpoints, daily temperature bin counts and endpoints, the rainfall threshold, the length of the long-run window used to compute deviations and z-scores, and the shape of the output data frame. This division of labor is useful: the command reduces the likelihood of programming errors in repetitive weather-processing tasks, but it does not substitute for careful research design.
